# Redraw at 2x, Keep Photos Home, Theme by Bundle

PawPixel's art looks chunky not because 11 screen pixels per art pixel is too many, but because only about **100 art pixels span the phone** where shipped pixel-art games put 320 to 480 across the screen; the cure is to double the source resolution of the pet (41x34 to 82x68) and the room (96 to 192 stage pixels), drawn natively by the existing procedural `Chibi` and `Room` code at an integer 5x, not to run an upscaler, because no scaler can keep a one-pixel outline at one pixel or know where a hand-placed anti-aliasing pixel belongs. Two rendering bugs make it worse today: `RoomBackdrop` and `PixelIcon` size their cells in dp, so on a 2.625-density phone a 3 dp room pixel is 7.875 device pixels and an 18 dp icon cell is 4.5, which nearest-neighbour turns into alternating fat and thin columns. PixelLab and its peers (Retro Diffusion, Scenario, Leonardo) are cloud-only, PixelLab's terms let it retain uploaded input images, and its animation skeleton has 18 human joints, so none of them belongs anywhere near the user's pet photo; they are worth a few dollars at build time to rough out furniture, tilesets and wall patterns that contain no user data, with outputs cached in the repo. The 12x12 icons should keep their two-tone ink but follow the small-icon craft rules (one-pixel strokes that join only diagonally, no doubles, jaggies or orphan pixels, one top-left light, a silhouette-row test) and be drawn only at multiples of 12 device pixels. For rooms, Animal Crossing's catalogue says a themed set is half 1x1 pieces, a third 2x1, a tenth 2x2 and almost nothing wider, anchored by two or three two-wide hero pieces and carried mostly by the wall and floor; PawPixel's four existing themes already fit that shape, and the next ones worth drawing are a bedroom, a kitchen and a bathroom. Games never apply a whole theme in one tap, but the one-tap bundle PawPixel already has (`RoomLayout.starter`) is the natural evolution of Animal Crossing's old ten-piece series with matching wall and floor and Stardew's named catalogues, and it should be presented as a previewed swap that keeps the pet's bed and bowl.

## Chunky means 100 art pixels across, not 11 screen pixels per block

The intuition that a bigger scale factor makes pixel art chunkier is only half right. Celeste renders at a constant 320x180 and reaches 1080p by integer-scaling 6x ([Steam community thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/504230/discussions/0/3832043350345268297)), 320x180 and 640x360 being "the most compatible low resolution for 16:9" because both integer-scale into every common monitor ([GDevelop forum](https://forum.gdevelop.io/t/pixel-perfect-is-not-pixel-perfect-in-fullscreen-mode/67927)); Stardew draws its "(mostly) 16x16" sprites ([Stardew forums](https://forums.stardewvalley.net/threads/sprite-size-first-time-modding-sv.7128/latest)) on what a zoom mod calls the game's "four-pixel art grid" ([True Zoom, CurseForge](https://www.curseforge.com/stardewvalley/mods/true-zoom)), so about 480 art pixels cross a 1920-wide screen. On a 24-inch 1080p monitor at roughly 92 ppi, Stardew's 4x block is about 1.1 mm wide and Celeste's 6x block 1.66 mm; PawPixel's 11x block on a 400 to 450 ppi phone is **0.62 to 0.70 mm**, physically smaller than either (arithmetic from the Android density formula, `px = dp * (dpi / 160)` ([Android, Support different pixel densities](https://developer.android.com/training/multiscreen/screendensities))). What the eye reads as chunky is therefore the count: PawPixel shows about **96 to 104 art pixels across a 1080-pixel phone** (`Room.kt` sets the box front at `(96 + 4) / GRID_W` and `LivePet.kt` picks `px = floor(1080 / 96) = 11`), against 320 to 480 in the games people compare it with. A Unity thread makes the same point from the other side: "chunky" comes from the physical size of the screen, a 640x360 image showing 3x3 blocks on a 15.6-inch 1080p laptop and 6x6 on a 4K screen of the same size ([Unity discussions](https://discussions.unity.com/t/scaling-pixel-art-and-other-2d-art-for-high-dpi-screens/708458)), and the Aseprite community's rule of thumb for phones is simply "at least 4x so pixels look intentional" ([Aseprite community](https://community.aseprite.org/t/how-should-i-design-pixel-art-for-unity-mobile-game/14991)).

Character source sizes point the same way. Pokémon's Generation III sprites are 64x64, Generations I and II 40 to 56 pixels ([Bulbapedia](https://bulbapedia.bulbagarden.net/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_sprite)), and the DS generations 96x96 ([PokémonDB](https://pokemondb.net/pokebase/377056/how-many-pixels-are-in-a-pokemon-sprite)); asset guides put detailed characters at 48 to 64 ([Wayline](https://www.wayline.io/learn/pixel-art/0)); Dead Cells skipped hand-drawing entirely by rendering 3D animations to pixel frames with an internal tool ([Habr, Vasseur](https://habr.com/post/416589)). PawPixel's 34-pixel-tall chibi sits at the GBA end of that range, and at 11x it is 374 device pixels tall, which is already the right on-screen size. The smallest change that moves it into shipped practice is to **double the canvas to 82x68 and halve the scale to 5 or 6x** (an 82x68 pet at 5x is 410x340 device pixels, nearly what it is now), with the rabbit's extra headroom doubled too and the image's own height used, as `Chibi` already requires. Because the room, props and pet must share one pixel size inside the diorama (see the mixels rule below), the stage doubles with it: a 192-wide stage gives `px = floor(1080 / 192) = 5`, 18x12 floor tiles in place of 9x6, and 24-pixel wall-slot steps in place of 12.

This is the moment the craft rules become usable, which they cannot be at 41x34. The most basic pixel-art line is one pixel wide and "the trick is to make the segments of the line only connect diagonally" ([Lospec, Pixel Art Outlines](https://lospec.com/articles/pixel-art-outlines)); doubles (a second stacked pixel that thickens a line into a 90-degree corner) and jaggies (uneven stair lengths) are the two faults that make an edge look broken ([Wayline lesson 2](https://www.wayline.io/learn/pixel-art/1)); anti-aliasing is placed "in corners" with mid-tones "spaced away from each other", by hand, never by a tool ([Tiny Warrior Games](https://tinywarriorgames.com/2018/12/14/game-development-pixel-art-anti-aliasing/)), and the Wesnoth rule is that a partially covered pixel takes the line's colour "by exact fraction of how much the line covers the pixel" ([Wesnoth wiki](https://wiki.wesnoth.org/How_to_anti-alias_sprite_art)); selective outlining replaces flat black with "a darker shade of the object it outlines", darker where the sprite is in shade and lighter where it is lit ([Lospec, Outlines part 2](https://lospec.com/articles/pixel-art-outlines-part-2-using-color)). At 2x density a one-pixel outline is 5 device pixels instead of 11, eyes can be two or three pixels with a one-pixel highlight, and the corner AA and selout pixels have somewhere to go. These rules are what make art read as "fine"; a scaler cannot add them because it does not know which 2x2 blocks were one stroke, and the procedural `Chibi` drawer does.

## Compose renders pixel-perfect only in device pixels, and two places still use dp

The engine rules are short and well documented. Compose's `DrawScope.drawImage` takes `dstOffset: IntOffset` and `dstSize: IntSize` in device pixels, so every scaled draw is a whole number of pixels; `filterQuality` defaults to `FilterQuality.Low`, documented as "the default behavior of bilinear filtering", and `FilterQuality.None` is the nearest-neighbour setting ("Fastest possible filtering ... Typically this implies nearest-neighbour filtering") ([DrawScope.kt](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/androidx/androidx/androidx-main/compose/ui/ui-graphics/src/commonMain/kotlin/androidx/compose/ui/graphics/drawscope/DrawScope.kt); [FilterQuality.kt](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/androidx/androidx/androidx-main/compose/ui/ui-graphics/src/commonMain/kotlin/androidx/compose/ui/graphics/FilterQuality.kt)). Unity's Pixel Perfect Camera spells out the rest: a fixed reference resolution, an integer zoom, sprites snapped to a 1/PPU grid so they "move in pixel-by-pixel increments", and black bars or more scene rather than a non-integer stretch ([Unity 2D Pixel Perfect manual](https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/com.unity.2d.pixel-perfect@5.1/manual/index.html)); sub-pixel movement is its own cause of uneven pixels, fixed by "restricting the sprite to whole coordinates" ([GDevelop forum](https://forum.gdevelop.io/t/pixel-perfect-is-not-pixel-perfect-in-fullscreen-mode/67927)). Because `dstSize` is an `IntSize`, an intended 10.5x is silently rounded for the whole image and nearest-neighbour then hands out the remainder as alternating 10- and 11-pixel columns; that artefact is the reason `floor` has to happen before multiplying, never after.

`LivePet.kt` already does this right: `pixelScale()` floors the ratio when it is at least 1, every draw passes `FilterQuality.None`, prop origins are `(p.x * px).roundToInt()`, and the pet's per-depth size `petPx = (g.size(depth) * px).roundToInt()` steps between integers as the pet walks between tile rows, which is correct pixel-art practice (a visible pop between integer scales beats the alternating-column shimmer). The deliberate exception, the depth-of-field band drawn with `FilterQuality.Medium`, is fine because it is meant to blur. Two places still break the rule. `RoomBackdrop` converts `pixel = 3.dp` with `pixel.toPx()`: on an xxhdpi phone that is 9 device pixels, but on a 420-dpi phone (density 2.625) it is **7.875**, so `drawImage` rounds the whole image and columns come out 7 and 8 wide in turn. `PixelIcon` divides the icon box by 12 to get a cell and uses `floor(i * cell)` edges, which avoids gaps but not unevenness: at the sizes used in the app, 18 dp on a 3x screen is 54 pixels and a **4.5-pixel cell**, 14 dp is 3.5, 9 dp is 2.25, and on a 2.625-density phone no dp size divides evenly at all (20 dp is 52.5 pixels, a 4.375 cell). Those are the "doubles" the craft rules warn about, produced by the renderer rather than the artist. The fix in both cases is to measure in pixels and derive dp from them: `floor(pixel.toPx())` for the backdrop, and for icons `k = round(desired.toPx() / 12)`, size `(12 * k).toDp()`, so a 20 dp icon becomes exactly 5x on a 3x screen and 4x on a 2.625 one. On iOS, Compose Multiplatform draws through Skia on the same path, so the same code holds; the native WidgetKit view must do its own nearest-neighbour (`.interpolation(.none)` on a SwiftUI `Image`), which is general knowledge rather than a fetched source because Apple's documentation could not be read for this research.

If a scaler is wanted at all, choose by what it does to the palette. Scale2x (EPX, AdvanceMAME 2001) increases "the size of small bitmaps guessing the missing pixels without blurring" and only copies neighbouring colours ([amadvance/scale2x](https://github.com/amadvance/scale2x)); its rule is four comparisons and four writes per source pixel, about thirty lines over an `IntArray`, and it keeps `PetLook`'s palette intact, but it rounds every outside corner and leaves shallow slopes stepped. hq2x analyses each 3x3 block with a 256-pattern lookup table, "suits images with clear, sharp edges, such as line graphics and cartoon sprites", and is LGPL ([grom358/hqx](https://github.com/grom358/hqx)); xBR "works by detecting edges and interpolating pixels along them" and is "specialized in 2D flat images" ([libretro forum](https://forums.libretro.com/t/xbr-algorithm-tutorial/123/21)); xBRZ, its successor whose "rule set is designed to preserve small image features", is GPLv3, integer factors only up to 6, with a Java port on Maven Central as `io.github.stanio:xbrz-core` ([xBRZ on SourceForge](https://sourceforge.net/projects/xbrz/); [Sonatype listing](https://central.sonatype.com/artifact/io.github.stanio/xbrz-core)). Eagle is unusable because "a single black pixel on a white background will vanish" (the eye highlight) ([Wikipedia, via search snippet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pixel-art_scaling_algorithms)). All of the interpolating family invent intermediate colours, which bloats the widget's mood PNGs and turns a flat two-tone chibi into a soft one, and emulator communities note that "running the smoothing algorithm on the individual sprite chunks would likely result in suboptimal results" ([TASVideos](https://tasvideos.org/Forum/Topics/18689)), which is exactly how `Room.scene()` hands pieces to the view. The only defensible stopgap is Scale2x applied uniformly to everything inside the diorama; scaling the pet alone and drawing it at half the room's pixel size would create mixels, the fault discussed next. No well-known pixel-art mobile game ships these scalers on purpose; emulators and remaster ports are the documented users. Redrawing at 82x68 wins.

## One grid inside the diorama; the chrome may use its own

The pixel-art community's name for mismatched pixel sizes is "mixels", "a pixel of incoherent or inconsistent size when compared to the rest of the artwork as a whole" ([ozdic](https://ozdic.com/word/mixel)); the rule from Michael Azzi's Pixel Logic is that "all the pixel art should fit on the same 'grid'" and "the outlines of the pixel art should have the exact same thickness" ([itch.io comment](https://itch.io/post/8448610)), backgrounds being the one tolerated exception and even then with one pixel size inside the background ([Flowlab community](https://community.flowlab.io/t/mini-rant-stop-using-mixels/38287)). Shipped games bend this at the UI boundary: Stardew exposes Zoom Level (75 to 200 percent) and UI Scale as independent options and tells modders to test with "zoom to maximum and UI scale to minimum" ([Stardew modder guide](https://wiki.stardewvalley.net/Modding:Modder_Guide/Game_Fundamentals)), so its scene and interface pixels differ in size by design. The compromise that respects both is the one PawPixel should adopt: **one pixel size for room, props, shadow and pet**, and a separate, smaller grid for the chrome (pills, dock, icons) that is consistent among its own elements. Pixel-art text at the scene's scale would be unusable, which is the pressure that produced Stardew's separate setting. The home card's `RoomBackdrop` at 3 dp and the live stage at 5x are two different grids on one screen; that is acceptable only because the card is a backdrop, and even there the pet drawn over it must use the card's pixel size, not its own.

For the 12x12 icons, the small-icon literature is consistent. Sixteen pixels is "the practical size" below which "most concepts stop being distinguishable" ([Divoom](https://divoom.com/blogs/industry-trends/pixel-art-still-works-4k-design-culture)), Lospec's editor defaults to a 16x16 canvas and hosts an 18x18 item-icon walkthrough ([Lospec Pixel Editor](https://apps.lospec.com/pixel-editor/); [Lospec tutorial listing](https://lospec.com/pixel-art-tutorials/lets-pixel-18x18-item-icons-by-heartbeast)), Stardew's cursor sheet is catalogued in 16x16 cells ([Stardew Modding Wiki, Cursors](https://stardewmodding.wiki.gg/wiki/Cursors)), and cozy UI packs advertise icons "optimized for 16x16 grid" with 9-slice panels ([itch.io tool UI pack](https://biruk-okami.itch.io/tool-ui-pack-pixel-art-icons-toolbar)). Twelve is at the small edge but workable, and it has one real advantage: a 24 dp touch target on a 3x screen is 72 pixels, an exact 6x, whereas 16x16 would need 4.5x. The rules to hold the set to: strokes one pixel wide; "more than five or six colors for a single 16-pixel sprite usually results in a blurry, muddy mess" ([Divoom, palette](https://divoom.com/blogs/setup-ideas/pixel-art-palette-how-many-colors)), so the existing ink-plus-light two-tone with a UI tint is already right; heavy black borders "vanish on dark backgrounds and look jagged on light ones", so prefer selective dark pixels "only where a shadow would naturally fall" ([Divoom](https://divoom.com/blogs/industry-trends/pixel-art-still-works-4k-design-culture)); light from the top-left, "the default for games and illustrations", kept consistent across the set ([Pixnote, Shading](https://pixnote.net/en/learn/shading)); diagonals drawn as a repeated 1-on/1-off pattern, "the same pattern for a given line thickness and angle" ([OpenGameArt thread](https://lpc.opengameart.org/node/113983)); no doubles, which often come from dragging a brush ([Clip Studio Tips](https://tips.clip-studio.com/de-de/articles/5360?org=1)), no jaggies, and no orphan pixels "poking off a shape with nothing connecting" ([Wayline](https://www.wayline.io/learn/pixel-art/1)); and the readability test is to "put your icon set in a row, look away, look back. Anything you have to think about has a silhouette problem" ([Divoom](https://divoom.com/blogs/industry-trends/pixel-art-still-works-4k-design-culture)). Inferred from those, a 12x12 glyph with a one-pixel margin has a 10x10 live area and room for three or four strokes across, which is enough for the bowl, drop, heart, paw, house, bell, gear and camera (distinctive outer silhouettes) and tight for the brush, pill, syringe and share arrow (thin shapes that depend on interior detail); the thin ones are the first to put through the row test. The current `DROP` glyph contains a Cyrillic `х` patched with `.replace('х', 'x')`, a reminder that the glyph strings deserve a unit test asserting every row is exactly 12 characters from the set `x`, `o`, `.`.

## PixelLab belongs in the build script, never in the photo path

PixelLab exposes a plain HTTPS JSON API with a Bearer token, base64 PNG in and out, per-call USD pricing of roughly **$0.006 to $0.013 per still image** (`create-image-bitforge` 32x32 $0.0071, 64x64 $0.00716; `image-to-pixelart` 64x64 $0.006; `create-image-pixflux` 64x64 $0.00793) and **$0.02 to $0.04 per animation clip** (`animate-with-text-v3` 32x32/4 frames $0.0221, 128x128/16 frames $0.0424) ([PixelLab API page](https://www.pixellab.ai/pixellab-api)); an official JS SDK, an official hosted MCP server with `create_character`, `animate_character`, `create_tileset` and `create_isometric_tile` tools ([pixellab-js](https://github.com/pixellab-code/pixellab-js); [pixellab-mcp](https://github.com/pixellab-code/pixellab-mcp)); and the v1 parameter catalogue mirrored by a third-party client, which shows that rotation and animation endpoints accept only square sizes of 16, 32, 64, 128 or 256, that bitforge returns a warning for sizes other than 16/32/64, and that errors are 401, 402, 422, 429 and 529 ([animus ENDPOINTS.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/railstracks/animus-package-pixellab/main/docs/ENDPOINTS.md)). The Terms of Service say the user owns the copyright of generations and may use them commercially (§1.3), that programmatic use is only through the official API (§2.2), that PixelLab reserves the right to change features, limits and pricing (§2.1), and, decisively, in §3.4 that PixelLab "may keep input and generated images for debugging or quality improvement" ([PixelLab ToS](https://www.pixellab.ai/termsofservice)). The photo-to-pixel route (`image-to-pixelart`, or bitforge with `init_image`) requires uploading the pet photo, so it is incompatible with PawPixel's promise that photos stay on the phone, and it would still need a proxy because the token is a secret and cannot ship in an APK or IPA.

The alternatives do not change the answer. Retro Diffusion's API is the most production-shaped of the group (queue-and-poll `POST /v2/inferences`, `Idempotency-Key`, free `check_cost` dry runs, refunds on failure, `rd_fast` $0.03 and `rd_plus` $0.06 per image, animations from $0.07) and its outputs are retrievable for 24 hours with request metadata kept 30 days ([Retro-Diffusion/api-examples](https://github.com/Retro-Diffusion/api-examples)); Scenario is pay-as-you-go "from $0.004 per image" with commercial use allowed on public models ([Scenario API pricing](https://docs.scenario.com/page/api-pricing); [Scenario commercial use](https://help.scenario.com/can-i-use-scenario-to-generate-images-for-commercial-projects)); Leonardo has no pixel-art-specific model that this research could find. Every one of them is cloud-only; none offers an on-device SDK. On-device diffusion exists but is the wrong tool: Apple's `ml-stable-diffusion` runs SD 2.1 at 512x512 in 7.9 to 15.4 seconds on iPhone 12 to 14 Pro Max with peak memory that "can exceed 2 GB" ([apple/ml-stable-diffusion](https://github.com/apple/ml-stable-diffusion)), MediaPipe's Android generator takes about 15 seconds on high-end phones ([Google Developers blog](https://developers.googleblog.com/en/mediapipe-on-device-text-to-image-generation-solution-now-available-for-android-developers/)), and the pixel-art LoRAs "require downscaling images 8 times using Nearest Neighbors" afterwards ([PromptLayer, pixel-art-xl](https://www.promptlayer.com/models/pixel-art-xl)), which gives no control over pixel count, palette or silhouette. The classic client-side pipeline, segment, posterize to a palette, nearest-neighbour downscale, optional dither and outline, is what Pixel It and Image-to-Pixel do in the browser ([giventofly/pixelit](https://github.com/giventofly/pixelit); [Tezumie/Image-to-Pixel](https://github.com/Tezumie/Image-to-Pixel)) and is essentially what `SpritePipeline` plus `Chibi` already does, deterministically and in milliseconds.

Consistency across poses, the one thing that might justify a vendor, is unproven and quadruped-hostile. PixelLab's skeleton has **18 human joints** (nose, neck, shoulders, elbows, hips, knees, eyes, ears) ([animus ENDPOINTS.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/railstracks/animus-package-pixellab/main/docs/ENDPOINTS.md)), a dog does not map onto it, and a community CLI's September 2026 commit states "the skeleton route does not return correct frames" with PixelLab recommending text animation instead ([pixellab-cli PR #33](https://github.com/protonspy/pixellab-cli/pull/33)); skeleton-v3 needs a Tier 1 subscription, 3 to 5 minutes per direction, and "a mismatch quietly costs transparency/color accuracy" ([pixelkiln issue #189](https://github.com/gfargo/pixelkiln/issues/189)). Sleep and eat are not template loops (templates are walk, run and idle), so they would go through text-described actions where identity drift is likeliest, and no independent benchmark of PixelLab versus Retro Diffusion on animals exists. At list price a full pet (base plus idle, walk, sleep and eat clips) is $0.10 to $0.20 per user before retries, which is cheap, but the latency is tens of seconds to minutes, unsuitable for an onboarding screen.

Where the vendors do earn their keep is development time. Furniture, wall patterns, floor tiles and whole-theme mood boards contain no user data, are explicitly commercial under PixelLab's terms (Retro Diffusion's own terms could not be found and should be read before relying on them), and cost cents: PixelLab bitforge at 32x32 or 64x64 with a `style_image` of an existing `Room` prop and `color_image` forcing the theme's palette, or Retro Diffusion `rd_plus` with `input_palette`, then Aseprite's `--batch` and Lua scripting to palette-lock, trim and sheet the output ([Aseprite CLI docs](https://www.aseprite.org/cli/)). Because the vendors' endpoints are partly beta, "pricing is provisional" on the image-to-pixel-art tool ([PixelLab docs](https://www.pixellab.ai/docs/tools/image-to-pixel-art)) and the ToS reserves the right to change limits, the right shape is a script under `scripts/` whose outputs are checked into the repo as reference PNGs that the developer then transcribes into `Room`'s procedural drawing calls, never a runtime dependency. The same arithmetic says a one-off $5 prepaid balance covers hundreds of reference pieces.

## Themed rooms are two hero pieces, filler, and mostly the wall and floor

The strongest quantitative guide to what belongs in a themed room is the Animal Crossing: New Horizons item dataset, which this research analysed locally from a JSON mirror: of **958 housewares, 475 are 1x1 (about half), 280 are 2x1 (29 percent), 106 are 2x2 (11 percent)** and everything three wide is under 5 percent; of 174 wall-mounted items, 112 are 1x1 and 29 are 2x1, and nothing wall-mounted is wider than two columns ([Norviah ACNH item mirror, Housewares](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Norviah/animal-crossing/master/json/data/Housewares.json); [Wall-mounted](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Norviah/animal-crossing/master/json/data/Wall-mounted.json)). Nookipedia confirms the footprint range of 1x0.5 to 3x3 ([Nookipedia, Furniture](https://nookipedia.com/wiki/Furniture)). Stardew's defaults are the same shape: couch sprites 3x2 on a 3x1 floor footprint, armchair 2x2, chair 1x2, table 2x3 ([Stardew Modding Wiki, custom furniture](https://stardewmodding.wiki.gg/wiki/Tutorial:_Making_Custom_Furniture_With_CP)), TVs 2x2 or 3x1 ([Stardew Valley Wiki, Plasma TV](https://stardewvalleywiki.com/Plasma_TV)). On PawPixel's 10x5 grid a three-wide piece takes 30 percent of the room's width, so the inference is at most one three-wide anchor per theme and rugs no larger than 3x2. The present catalogue already follows this: 44 pieces, of which only the bed, rug, sofa, play tunnel, pond, window and shelf are three wide, and only the bunting and string lights (wall pieces) four.

What the dataset says about each theme maps cleanly onto `RoomTheme`. ACNH's living-room concept is anchored by a 2x1 sofa, a 2x1 low table, a 1x1 retro TV or 2x1 stereo, a 2x1 fireplace, a 1x1 floor lamp, a 2x1 shelf and a plant, over parquet, herringbone or pine-board floors and painted-wood or moulded-panel walls, with 3x3 to 5x5 rugs; the child's-room concept has a 2x2 train set, 2x2 kids' tent, 2x1 slide, 1x1 rocking horse, 1x1 blocks, 2x1 wooden-block bench and chest, 2x1 dreamy or race-car bed, over puzzle, block or dot floors and playroom, quilt or heart-pattern walls; the garden concept has 3x3 gazebos and ponds, 2x2 trees and plastic pools, 2x1 benches, wagons and pond stones, and 1x1 birdhouses, lanterns, bird baths, scarecrows and a **doghouse**, over lawn, meadow, gravel and dirt floors and fence, lattice and bamboo-grove walls ([Housewares.json](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Norviah/animal-crossing/master/json/data/Housewares.json); [Floors.json](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Norviah/animal-crossing/master/json/data/Floors.json); [Wallpaper.json](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Norviah/animal-crossing/master/json/data/Wallpaper.json)). Cozy Grove sells the same idea as seven-item sets (the Relaxing Campground set is a folding chair, hammock, park sign, picnic, s'mores table, water pump and wood shed) grouped under theme names such as Rustic ([Cozy Grove wiki](https://cozygrove.fandom.com/wiki/Category:Relaxing_campground); [Decoration](https://cozygrove.fandom.com/wiki/Decoration_(Cozy_Grove))). PawPixel's living room (sofa, TV, coffee table, cushion, record player), playroom (toy chest, blocks, tunnel, ball pit, rocking horse, tent, bunting, chalkboard) and garden (flower bed, bench, lantern, bird bath, pond, tree, birdhouse, wind chime) are each already within a few pieces of the reference anchors; the gaps worth drawing are a 2x1 fireplace and a 1x1 floor-standing bookshelf variant for the living room, a 2x1 slide, a 2x2 train set and a play-mat rug for the playroom, and for the garden a fence wall-row, a 1x1 doghouse (the one item in the whole dataset built for a pet), a 2x1 garden wagon and a 1x1 scarecrow.

The next themes are chosen by the same data. ACNH has no bedroom concept but 47 bed-tagged pieces (2x2 wooden double and log beds, 2x1 simple, ironwood and dreamy beds) with 1x1 end tables, wardrobes, closets and mirrors; its kitchen concept is 2x2 islands, 2x1 counters, stoves and freezers, 1x1 fridges and ranges, a 2x1 pot rack on the wall and 2x1 kitchen mats, over retro or restaurant-kitchen floors and subway-tile or brick walls; its bathroom is a 2x2 whirlpool or 2x1 claw-foot tub, 1x1 toilet, sink, stool, scale and toy duck, a 1x1 towel rack on the wall and 2x1 bath mats, over honeycomb, mosaic and aqua tile; its den and office are 2x1 desks, 1x1 chairs, cardboard boxes in 1x1 to 2x2, a 2x1 corkboard or bulletin board, a 1x1 wall clock and tabletop books, typewriters and lamps, over rosewood floors and library walls; and its café is 2x2 bistro tables, 2x1 counters and dessert cases, 1x1 counter chairs and shop signs, a 2x1 bottle shelf on the wall and 2x1 entrance mats, over coffee-shop, diner and brick walls ([Housewares.json](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Norviah/animal-crossing/master/json/data/Housewares.json); [Miscellaneous.json](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Norviah/animal-crossing/master/json/data/Miscellaneous.json); [Rugs.json](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Norviah/animal-crossing/master/json/data/Rugs.json)). For a pet-care app the bedroom (the pet already sleeps) and the kitchen (the pet already eats) are the two that reinforce existing `Use.SLEEP` and `Use.EAT` behaviour, with the bathroom a natural home for the grooming and bath tasks; the study and café are fourth and fifth.

Palettes follow from the earlier research plus the dataset's wall-and-floor cues. The cozy genre's signature is earth tones ("yellow ochre and warm browns", Stardew being the type specimen) ([Krzywinska et al.](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/15554120241310920)); the practical recipe is 16 to 32 colours with a cool, desaturated base and warm hues reserved for light sources ([BudgetPixel](https://budgetpixel.com/blog/how-to-create-a-cozy-pixel-art-scene-in-5-steps)); ramps shift hue about 20 degrees per step, highlights toward yellow and shadows toward blue ([the-pixel.art](https://the-pixel.art/articles/hue-shifting-pixel-art-shading/)); and a 16x16 piece gets two shading stages, a 32x32 piece three ([Pixnote, Shading](https://pixnote.net/en/learn/shading)). The existing `DAY_BY_THEME` palettes (sage wall and walnut floor for the living room, butter wall and foam-blue floor for the playroom, hedge wall and grass floor for the garden) already match the dataset's material cues; the inferred palettes for the new themes are a bedroom of birch or pine-board floor with a painted-wood or soft-panel wall and a 3x2 rug, a kitchen of checker or retro tile floor with a subway-tile or white-brick wall and a 2x1 mat, and a bathroom of honeycomb or mosaic tile floor with an aqua-tile wall and a 2x1 bath mat. Three things carry across all themes: a palette `copy` that overrides wall, floor and rug while keeping the wood, fabric and metal ramps, so a bowl is the same bowl in every room; a night variant produced by the existing `dim` toward deep blue; and the earlier report's rule that furniture outlines are a darker shade of each piece's own hue with no outline on flat pieces.

Games present a theme switch as a pair, not a bundle. In New Horizons wallpaper and flooring are inventory items applied from decorating mode (d-pad down, then X for the inventory), sold daily by Nook's Cranny and Saharah ([Twinfinite](https://twinfinite.net/guides/animal-crossing-new-horizons-wallpapers-rugs-change/)), with accent walls applied to one side of the room once the Pro Decorating License is held ([Animal Crossing Wiki](https://animalcrossing.fandom.com/wiki/Furniture_(New_Horizons))); Happy Home Paradise previews a feature wall by rotating the room to face it and cycles an item's colour variants with X, with separate lighting and soundscape menus ([Real Homes](https://www.realhomes.com/features/how-to-design-a-room-around-a-statement-piece-in-happy-home-paradise)); earlier Animal Crossing games sold a series as "roughly 10 furniture pieces with matching wallpaper and flooring", a structure New Horizons dropped ([Nookipedia, Furniture](https://nookipedia.com/wiki/Furniture)), though series such as Moroccan (four walls, four floors), bamboo and cherry blossoms still ship matched wall, floor and rug triples ([Wallpaper.json](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Norviah/animal-crossing/master/json/data/Wallpaper.json)); Stardew sells themes as named catalogues (Furniture, Wizard, Junimo, Joja, Retro) whose items cost nothing once owned ([Furniture Catalogue](https://stardewvalleywiki.com/Furniture_Catalogue); [Retro Catalogue](https://stardewvalleywiki.com/Retro_Catalogue)) and shows green and red placement cells ([Stardew Valley Wiki, Furniture](https://stardewvalleywiki.com/Furniture)); Cozy Grove tags each decoration with a set and a theme ([Cozy Grove wiki](https://cozygrove.fandom.com/wiki/Decoration_(Camp_Spirit))); Unpacking fixes walls, floor and built-ins per level and lets the player place only items ([Wikipedia, Unpacking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unpacking_(video_game))). None of them applies a whole theme in one tap, which makes PawPixel's `RoomLayout.starter(theme)` an improvement rather than a copy, provided the presentation borrows the three affordances players already know: a preview of wall plus floor plus the anchor pieces together before committing (Happy Home Paradise's feature-wall preview, Finch's preview-in-room), the pet's bed and bowl staying where they were so the switch never moves the things the pet uses, and the pieces that fit any room (`theme == null`) carrying over unchanged. Because themes are free and nothing is ever taken away, the picker reads as a wardrobe for the room, which is the Finch pattern reviewers praise.

## Conclusion

The research collapses what looked like three separate questions into one discipline: pixel density. The pet looks chunky because its canvas is half the size convention expects, the icons shimmer because their cells are not whole device pixels, and a room theme reads as cheap when its pieces are drawn on a different grid from the pet. Doubling the source canvas and measuring everything in device pixels fixes all three at once, and it makes the craft rules (one-pixel strokes, selout, hand-placed corner AA, a 20-degree hue shift per ramp step) applicable for the first time, which is where "fine" actually comes from. The AI question answers itself once the privacy constraint is taken seriously: a vendor that may retain input images and models characters as 18-joint humans has nothing to offer a quadruped drawn from a photo that must never leave the phone, but at cents per image it is a good sketchbook for furniture and tiles that a developer then draws for real.

The product implication is that PawPixel's procedural pipeline, which looked like a limitation next to PixelLab's demos, is its asset. Everything inside the diorama is drawn from a few palettes and a few drawing routines, so doubling the resolution, adding a bedroom, re-ramping every piece's three tones, or lighting a theme for night is a code change rather than an art re-commission, and the house style stays one hand. The room themes should be built as the earlier research recommended for furniture generally: bundles the pet visibly uses, previewed before they are applied, never sold, never timed, and shared with the household as one string.
