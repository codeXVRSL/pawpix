# Engineering notes: procedural 2:1 isometric (and oblique) pixel room with movable furniture on a ~150x110 canvas

Scope: plain Kotlin drawing into an `IntArray` pixel buffer that is nearest-neighbour upscaled on the phone. No art files. Everything below is either a cited fact (with URL) or a derivation marked as an inference. Research caveat: during this session the fetch tool resolved only github.com; every other host (clintbellanger.net, slynyrd.com, code.tutsplus.com, redblobgames.com, wikibooks, gamedev.net archive, forum.godotengine.org, pixelparmesan.com) returned DNS failures, so for those sources the findings rest on search-result excerpts rather than the full page. Primary material read in full: Shaun Lebron's *IsometricBlocks* tutorial (GitHub) and the Habbo-compatible `nitro-renderer` `RoomGeometry.ts` (GitHub).

Conventions used throughout the Inferences: tile coordinates `(tx, ty)` with `(0,0)` the back corner (top of the diamond floor), `tx` increasing toward the lower-right, `ty` toward the lower-left; tile diamond `W x H` with `W = 2H` and `H` even; `O = (ox, oy)` is the screen position (in canvas pixels) of the top vertex of tile `(0,0)`.

## Key question 1: Exact formulas (tile to screen, inverse, seam-free diamond rasterisation)

### Takeaway
The standard 2:1 formulas are `screenX = (tx - ty) * W/2`, `screenY = (tx + ty) * H/2`, inverted as `tx = sx/W + sy/H`, `ty = sy/H - sx/W` (plus an origin / half-tile correction that is the usual source of "off by half a tile" bugs). A diamond fill that tests pixel centres against the diamond inequality partitions the plane exactly for any even `H`, so adjacent tiles neither overlap nor leave seams, and its edges are automatically 2-pixel 2:1 staircases.

### Cited Findings
- Cartesian-to-isometric: isometric x is the difference of the 2D coordinates and isometric y is half their sum; `(100,100) -> (0,100)`, `[10,5] -> [5,7.5]` — [Tuts+, Creating Isometric Worlds (Spanish mirror)](https://code.tutsplus.com/creando-mundos-isometricos-una-guia-para-desarrolladores-de-juegos--gamedev-6511t). Clint Bellanger's isometric math article is cited by the same tutorial as "the best technical explanation" of what isometric projection means, and by a GameDev.net poster as "one of the best resources" on the subject — [GameDev.net thread](https://gamedev.net/forums/topic/714691-isometric-tiling); the article itself (clintbellanger.net/articles/isometric_math/) could not be fetched in this session.
- Baseline inverse for flat diamond tiles: `map.x = screen.x / TILE_WIDTH + screen.y / TILE_HEIGHT; map.y = screen.y / TILE_HEIGHT - screen.x / TILE_WIDTH` — [GameDev.net, "isometric mouse coordinates"](https://gamedev.net/forums/topic/642767-i39m-desperate-isometric-mouse-coordinates).
- The half-tile problem: one tutorial shifts the click by half a tile so it lands on a tile centre, divides by the tile dimensions, and "uses doubles and rounds the result, since integer maths will fail due to needing to round half up"; another subtracts half the tile height from the click y before flooring — [Parallel Realities isometric tutorial](https://parallelrealities.co.uk/tutorials/isometric/isometric2.php); [Silverlight blog, 2009](https://dcmpx.remotevs.com/com/blogspot/betablending/PL/2009). A GameDev.net reply uses a screen-centre offset "typically screen width divided by 2, minus half the tile's width" and divides by a combined `w*h/2` denominator — [GameDev.net](https://gamedev.net/forums/topic/642767-i39m-desperate-isometric-mouse-coordinates/5059808/).
- A 32x16 2:1 tile grid is laid out by "stepping right 16 pixels, down 8"; the classic article notes "a good angle to view uses a 2:1 ratio" and that its example tile height is 15 rather than 16 because it used 2.1:1 — [GameDev.net archive, "Isometric Views" (article744)](https://archive.gamedev.net/archive/reference/articles/article744.html).
- The 2:1 pixel slope is approximately 26.565 degrees rather than 30 degrees, chosen because a true 30-degree line "would look awfully jaggy for pixel art" — [Clip Studio Tips, Isometric View Pixel Art Style](https://tips.clip-studio.com/zh-tw/articles/4969?org=1); same point in [SLYNYRD Pixelblog 41](https://slynyrd.com/blog/2022/11/28/pixelblog-41-isometric-pixel-art) (2:1 sits "close to 26.5°").
- "For every 2 pixels you move horizontally, you move 1 pixel vertically"; the full viewable iso tile at the smallest common size is 16x8 — [sprite-ai.art isometric guide](https://www.sprite-ai.art/guides/isometric-pixel-art); [OpenGameArt forum](https://opengameart.org/node/5374).
- Seams: "a single pixel of misalignment between tile corners creates visible seams"; tile corners must sit at exact points — [sprite-ai.art](https://www.sprite-ai.art/guides/isometric-pixel-art). The pragmatic fix used by artists is to make tiles slightly bigger so the alpha edge is covered when tiling — [OpenGameArt, Making Tiles](https://opengameart.org/content/making-tiles); a Godot thread suggests oversizing tiles with a half-transparent one-pixel fringe and notes that "nearest" filtering fixes the lines for pixel art — [Godot forum](https://forum.godotengine.org/t/thin-lines-between-2d-isometric-tiles-nearest-fixes-pixel-art-but-no-filter-works-for-high-fidelity-tiles/119606).
- Tile top/bottom edge styles: three styles exist, and "the four-pixel version [is] the best one for game tiling" — [Clip Studio Tips](https://tips.clip-studio.com/zh-tw/articles/4969?org=1). SLYNYRD describes a cube style "D1" that "adheres more to the 2:1 grid and tiles perfectly, but those sharp corners are unnerving" — [SLYNYRD Pixelblog 41](https://slynyrd.com/blog/2022/11/28/pixelblog-41-isometric-pixel-art).
- Habbo-compatible renderer geometry (nitro-renderer `RoomGeometry.ts`): `SCALE_ZOOMED_IN = 64`, `SCALE_ZOOMED_OUT = 32`; the scale setter stores `scale * Math.sqrt(0.5)`; z is compressed by `_z_scale_internal = sqrt(1/2) / sqrt(3/4)` (≈ 0.8165); screen x/y are dot products of the (x,y,z) offset with rotated basis vectors times `_scale`, and depth is the dot product with a depth vector, rejected outside `[-500, 500]` — [billsonnn/nitro-renderer RoomGeometry.ts](https://github.com/billsonnn/nitro-renderer/blob/dev/src/room/utils/RoomGeometry.ts).
- Lebron's projection for blocks with height: `isoX = x + z; isoY = y + z; h = (isoX - isoY) * cos(pi/6); v = (isoX + isoY) / 2` (true 30-degree iso, not 2:1) — [shaunlebron/IsometricBlocks index.htm](https://github.com/shaunlebron/IsometricBlocks/blob/master/index.htm).

### Inferences (derived; verify with a unit test)
- **Tile to screen (anchor = top vertex of tile (0,0) at `O`).** For integer tile `(tx, ty)`, the top vertex of its diamond is
  `sx = ox + (tx - ty) * (W/2)`, `sy = oy + (tx + ty) * (H/2)`; its centre is `(sx, sy + H/2)`. The same formula with `Float` tile coordinates gives the screen point of any floor position, which is what the walking pet uses (see Q6). Height above the floor is subtracted from `sy` only: `screenY -= zPixels` (a 2:1 iso has no horizontal component for height). This is Lebron's `isoX = x + z` idea restated for 2:1: in 2:1, moving up `z` tile-units is `z * H` pixels straight up.
- **Screen to tile (continuous, then floor).** Using pixel centres `(px + 0.5, py + 0.5)`:
  `a = (px + 0.5 - ox) / (W/2)`, `b = (py + 0.5 - oy) / (H/2)`, then `fx = (a + b) / 2`, `fy = (b - a) / 2`, `tx = floor(fx)`, `ty = floor(fy)`. With `W = 2H` this equals the cited forum formula `tx = dx/W + dy/H`, `ty = dy/H - dx/W` where `(dx, dy)` is the offset from `O`; the cited "half tile" corrections exist because those authors anchored the origin at the tile's top-left bounding-box corner instead of the top vertex. Anchoring at the vertex removes the correction. Pixel centres (the `+0.5`) are what keeps the inverse consistent with the fill rule below.
- **Seam-free diamond fill.** Pixel `(x, y)` belongs to the diamond with centre `(cx, cy)` (integers `cx = sx + W/2`, `cy = sy + H/2`) iff `|x + 0.5 - cx| / (W/2) + |y + 0.5 - cy| / (H/2) < 1`. Proof that no pixel centre ever lies on the boundary (so `<` vs `<=` never matters and neighbours partition the plane): with `W = 2H`, boundary means `|2a+1| + 2|2b+1| = 2H` for integers `a, b`; the left side is odd and the right side even. Neighbour centres differ by `(±W/2, ±H/2)`, which is integer iff `H` is even. Hence: **use `H` even (16x8, 20x10, 24x12, 32x16); never 18x9 or 14x7.** Row form for a loop: for row `y` in `0 until H`, `dy = |y + 0.5 - H/2|`, `hw = H - 2*dy`, fill `x` from `ceil(H - hw - 0.5)` to `floor(H + hw - 0.5)` inclusive (relative to the tile's bounding-box left/top). For 16x8 this yields widths 2, 6, 10, 14, 14, 10, 6, 2 (columns 7..8, 5..10, 3..12, 1..14, ...), i.e. the two-pixel-tip diamond whose outermost columns 0 and 15 are filled by the neighbouring tiles' tips. The edge therefore advances 2 px per row: the 2:1 staircase for free.
- **Which corner style to use.** The "four-pixel top" tile that artists prefer for tiling (cited above) overlaps its neighbour by two pixels when stepped `(W/2, H/2)`, so as a *fill rule* it needs an explicit draw order (later tiles overwrite). The two-pixel-tip rule above needs no order. If the look of the four-pixel tip is wanted for *outlined* tiles, draw the fill with the partition rule and then draw grid lines separately (1 px, in a slightly darker floor shade) along the diamond edges using the same pixel-centre test with tolerance; lines drawn along `y = c ± x/2` with integer stepping of 2 px per row are exactly 2:1 and never jaggy.
- **Floor grid lines.** Instead of per-tile outlines, draw the two families of parallel 2:1 lines once across the whole floor: for `k in 0..n`, the line through `O + (k*W/2, k*H/2)` with slope `-1/2`, and the line through `O + (-k*W/2, k*H/2)` with slope `+1/2`, each from edge to edge of the floor diamond, clipped. One line per family per `k` means shared edges are drawn once and cannot double up (a common cause of uneven line thickness when each tile draws its own outline).
- **Floating point is not needed.** All the above work in integers if you multiply through: the inequality becomes `2*H*|2(x - cx) + 1| + ... ` style; practically, `Int` maths with `W/2`, `H/2` integers and the `+0.5` folded into `2x + 1` is exact.

### Gaps
- Bellanger's article, SLYNYRD's posts, the Tuts+ primer and Red Blob Games could not be fetched; the formulas attributed to them above come from search excerpts and from independent derivation. Red Blob Games has no isometric article I could confirm; the site did not resolve.
- No source gives a published, proven "seam-free diamond fill" algorithm; the rule above is a derivation (with a parity argument) rather than a cited algorithm.

## Key question 2: Painter's order for mixed footprints, heights, wall-mounted objects and stacked objects

### Takeaway
A single sort key (`x+y`, `x-y`, screen y) is provably insufficient once footprints differ in size; the correct general method for axis-aligned boxes is Lebron's pairwise separating-axis test (x, then y, then z) followed by a topological sort, which also handles stacking (bowl on table) through the z test. For a room of a dozen pieces the O(n²) pairwise test is negligible. Walls and wall-mounted items form a fixed back layer drawn before the floor objects; multi-tile furniture that is drawn procedurally can alternatively be split into per-tile slices and sorted by `x+y`.

### Cited Findings
- Sorting by `X - Y` worked for square objects but "a 1x2 object made the sorting function misbehave" — [GameDev.net](https://gamedev.net/forums/topic/425769/).
- A practical blog recipe: one sort key `grid x + y` plus a small height term, so nearer cells draw last; tiles and entities go into one list and are sorted once, so a character can be in front of one tile and behind another — [bugnet.io](https://bugnet.io/blog/how-to-fix-isometric-tilemap-sprite-depth-sort-overlap). Another community answer: "add together the x and y values then add one to produce the z value" — [GameDev StackExchange mirror](https://backiee.wasmer.app/http_gamedev_stackexchange_com/a/73470).
- Multi-cell objects: with multi-cell objects "you cannot use y sorting at all anymore"; the recommended fix (from a Unity article) is to split the object into single-tile parts — [Godot forum](https://forum.godotengine.org/t/unable-to-properly-y-sort-entities-in-a-multi-leveled-isometric-tilemap/93061); a Corona forum poster notes everything they found online was O(n²), slow for 100+ objects — [Solar2D forum](https://forums.solar2d.com/t/isometric-depth-sorting-in-corona/325750).
- Lebron's exact method (read in full). Screen-space overlap test: each box's silhouette is a hexagon with sides parallel to the vertical and the two diagonals; "Hexagons overlap if and only if all axis regions overlap" (screen x, screen y and an added horizontal axis h). Front/back decision for two non-intersecting boxes, with the convention that lower x and lower y are nearer the camera and higher z is nearer: test x first (if `box1.minX >= box2.maxX`, box1 is behind; if `box2.minX >= box1.maxX`, box1 is in front), then the same on y, then z (if `box1.minZ >= box2.maxZ`, box1 is in front; if `box2.minZ >= box1.maxZ`, box1 is behind). The function falls through (undefined) when the boxes overlap on all three axes, i.e. when they intersect in space. Draw order: each box keeps a list of the boxes behind it; a box is drawn only after everything behind it, which is a topological sort of the "behind" graph (described as essentially a depth-first search). Cycles are possible (three boxes each partly in front of another) and "need to be split or handled with clipping"; the appendix points to Tarjan's SCC algorithm — [shaunlebron/IsometricBlocks](https://github.com/shaunlebron/IsometricBlocks/blob/master/index.htm). A Construct forum post describes the same "filmation math" technique: compare positions and sizes of any two visually overlapping blocks to decide which is drawn first, with the caveat that it fails if objects overlap in iso space or if there is a loop — [construct.net post](https://construct.net/en/users/16516/r0j0hound/forum-posts/page-475).
- Fixed scenery first: an OpenApoc rendering thread describes per-cell order "floor, left wall, right wall, object in tile" drawn first, then movable objects sorted by Z, then the next height level — [r/OpenApoc mirror](https://pol1.lr.ggtyler.dev/r/OpenApoc/comments/aabwru/rendering_engine). Unity's isometric tilemap docs: "Tiles placed further to the 'back' of the Tilemap need to be rendered first", and the default sort axis `(0,1,0)` ignores Z, so they suggest a bias like `(0,1,-0.26)` — [Unity Manual, Isometric Tilemaps](https://docs.unity3d.com/es/2021.1/Manual/Tilemap-Isometric-CreateIso.html).
- Combined floor+wall sprites (Kenney building tiles) are the hard case: sorting by y alone hides objects that should be on top, and a z-index fixes some and breaks others; "there is no escape from obscuring some part of the world" with a pure screen-order approach — [GameDev.net, isometric tile render order (2025)](https://gamedev.net/forums/topic/719280-isometric-tile-render-order).
- The Habbo-compatible renderer computes depth as a dot product of the scaled (x,y,z) offset with a depth vector derived from the camera angles, rather than from a tile-sum key — [nitro-renderer RoomGeometry.ts](https://github.com/billsonnn/nitro-renderer/blob/dev/src/room/utils/RoomGeometry.ts).

### Inferences (recommended procedure)
- **Layer order for the room.** (1) Back-left wall, then back-right wall (they never overlap; the shared vertical edge belongs to one of them). (2) Wall-mounted items painted directly onto the wall layer (picture frame, window, wall shelf back-plate, clock), ordered among themselves only by their vertical position (lower first) which only matters if they overlap. (3) The whole floor: every tile diamond, grid lines, skirting (a 2-3 px band at the base of each wall, drawn as part of the wall or as the first floor row), the rug and the flat shadows. None of these occlude anything of height, so no sorting is needed. (4) Floor objects (furniture, pet) in topological order from Lebron's test. (5) Optional overlays (speech bubble, selection outline).
- **Why a sum key fails even for the simple room (worked counter-examples).** Convention: higher `x+y` is nearer. A = 1x3 object at tiles `x=0, y=0..2`, B = 1x1 at `(1,0)`. Lebron's x test: `B.minX (1) >= A.maxX (1)` so A is behind B. Key `minX+minY`: A=0, B=1 draws A first (correct). Now A = 3x1 at `x=0..2, y=1`, B = 1x1 at `(1,0)`: y test says B is behind A; `minX+minY` is 1 for both (tie, undefined). Key `maxX+maxY`: first case A=4, B=3 draws B first (wrong). So neither corner sum works for both; this is the GameDev.net "1x2 object" failure in concrete form.
- **Adapting Lebron's test to this convention.** In this note's axes (tile `(0,0)` at the back, higher `tx`, `ty` nearer): A is behind B if `A.maxX <= B.minX` or `A.maxY <= B.minY`; A is in front of B if `B.maxX <= A.minX` or `B.maxY <= A.minY`; otherwise fall back to z: A in front if `A.minZ >= B.maxZ`, behind if `B.minZ >= A.maxZ`. Only run the test for pairs whose screen bounding boxes intersect (Lebron's hexagon overlap test, or simply the rectangular bounds of the drawn pixels, which is conservative). Kotlin sketch:
  ```kotlin
  data class Box(val x0: Float, val y0: Float, val x1: Float, val y1: Float, val z0: Int, val z1: Int) // tiles, tiles, px, px
  fun behind(a: Box, b: Box): Boolean? = when {
      a.x1 <= b.x0 || a.y1 <= b.y0 -> true
      b.x1 <= a.x0 || b.y1 <= a.y0 -> false
      a.z0 >= b.z1 -> false
      b.z0 >= a.z1 -> true
      else -> null // intersecting boxes: forbid by placement rules
  }
  // order: for each i, behindList[i] = { j : behind(j, i) == true && screenBoundsOverlap(i, j) }; DFS post-order
  ```
  With n <= ~20 objects this is at most 190 pair tests per redraw.
- **Avoiding cycles and the undefined case by construction.** The room editor should snap furniture to whole tiles, reject footprints that overlap on the floor (unless one object is declared "on top of" the other), and keep objects inside the floor. Under those rules boxes never intersect, so `behind` never returns `null`; cycles of the kind Lebron shows need L-shaped overlaps of tall pieces, which snapped rectangular footprints that don't overlap make impossible in practice (not proven here; add an assertion in debug builds).
- **Objects on surfaces (bowl on table).** Give the bowl the table's footprint (or a sub-rectangle of it) and `z0 = tableHeightPx`. The x and y tests then fall through and the z test puts the bowl in front. Alternatively make it a composite: the table's draw routine draws its top face and then immediately draws the bowl; composites are simplest when the child never needs to be picked separately.
- **Wall-mounted objects.** Treat the picture frame and window as wall decoration (layer 2, no footprint). A shelf that protrudes needs a footprint one tile deep against the wall (`ty = 0..1` for the right wall or `tx = 0..1` for the left wall) and `z0 > 0`; any floor object in front of that tile row sorts in front via the y (or x) test, while the shelf's back-plate is still painted on the wall. The Kenney/GameDev.net problem (wall and floor in one sprite) does not arise when walls and floor are drawn procedurally as separate layers.
- **Splitting as a cheaper alternative.** Because every piece is drawn procedurally, a 2x1 bed can be drawn as two 1x1 iso boxes (same colours, no outline on the shared edge) and the whole room sorted by `tx + ty` with height as a tiebreak, which is the bugnet / Godot "split" approach. This is exact for 1x1 columns of any height on a flat floor and costs one sort. It breaks for objects that overhang (a table top wider than its legs), so prefer Lebron's test unless all furniture is "full column" shaped.
- **The pet.** Insert the pet as a box of footprint `[px - 0.35, px + 0.35] x [py - 0.35, py + 0.35]` (tiles, fractional) and height = sprite height in px so it sorts like furniture. Keep the pet's wander positions on tiles not covered by furniture footprints (a boolean walkable mask), which also guarantees its box never intersects a furniture box.

### Gaps
- No source documents Habbo's actual z-order formula; the renderer read uses a camera-angle depth vector, and the scuti-renderer / chroma4j pages reached had no ordering code. The common community statement that Habbo uses `x + y` sorting could not be confirmed.
- Lebron's tutorial leaves the "boxes intersect on all three axes" case undefined and gives no detail on applying Tarjan's algorithm to cycles.

## Key question 3: Procedural shading of iso boxes and outline rules

### Takeaway
Sources agree the three faces get three distinct values and that the light direction decides which is which: with an overhead light the top is brightest; with a top-left light one classic guide makes the left face brightest, the top second and the right face darkest. Pick one light for the whole room and keep it fixed. Outline rules were not found in a fetchable source; the recommendation below is an inference.

### Cited Findings
- Northlake guide: with the light at the top left, "One side (the one directly facing the light) is the brightest, the second brightest is the top of the box (because the light is coming from above) and the last side is the darkest" — [The Complete Guide to Isometric Pixel Art, ch. 3.2](https://students.northlake.wa.edu.au/StudentHTML/IsoPixArt/chapter3point2.html).
- Overhead-light scheme: "a lit top, a mid-tone right side and a shaded left side" — [uxpilot voxel cubes](https://uxpilot.ai/labs/dither-generator/voxel-cubes); a gallery piece describes "the top face appears brightest, suggesting an overhead light source, while the side faces are rendered with darker tones" — [pixie.haus](https://pixie.haus/pixelart/574813/isometric-gray-cube-game-asset).
- Wikibooks' advice on which face gets which tone: "Eyeball it and find out what looks right to you" — [Wikibooks, Isometric Pixel Art / Making a Box](https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Isometric_Pixel_Art/Making_a_Box).
- SLYNYRD's cube styles: style D1 follows the 2:1 grid and tiles perfectly but has sharp corners the author dislikes; a single 2:1 guide line kept on a layer is used to check alignment — [Pixelblog 41](https://slynyrd.com/blog/2022/11/28/pixelblog-41-isometric-pixel-art), [Pixelblog 54](https://www.slynyrd.com/blog/2025/1/23/pixelblog-54-isometric-pixel-art).

### Inferences (recommended procedure)
- **One light for the room: top-left-front.** Then: top face = base lightened (e.g. +20 to +30 % lightness), left face = base, right face = base darkened (-25 to -35 %). This matches the floor-plan reading that the back-left wall is lit and the back-right wall is in shade, which also helps separate the two walls. If instead the brand look wants "top brightest, left mid, right darkest" (the overhead scheme), use the same three values with left and top swapped; either way the right face is darkest in both cited schemes, so keep that constant.
- **Drawing an iso box procedurally (footprint `x0..x1`, `y0..y1` tiles, height `h` px).** Project the four floor corners: `B = P(x0, y0)` (back vertex), `R = P(x1, y0)` (right), `F = P(x1, y1)` (front/bottom), `L = P(x0, y1)` (left), where `P` is the tile-to-screen formula from Q1 using the vertex (not centre). Front edges: left face top edge from `L` to `F`: `yF(x) = L.y + (x - L.x) / 2`; right face: from `F` to `R`: `yF(x) = F.y - (x - F.x) / 2`. Back edges: `yB(x) = L.y - (x - L.x) / 2` for `x < B.x`, `yB(x) = B.y + (x - B.x) / 2` for `x >= B.x`. Then per screen column `x` from `L.x` to `R.x - 1` (evaluating at `x + 0.5`): top face is `y` with `yB - h <= y + 0.5 < yF - h`; side face is `yF - h <= y + 0.5 < yF`, coloured left if `x + 0.5 < F.x` else right. Evaluating at pixel centres with half-open intervals gives contiguous faces with no gaps or double rows, and the top-face edges are 2:1 staircases. Column-wise filling also makes clipping to the canvas trivial (`clamp` the y range, skip columns outside `0 until canvasW`).
- **Outlines.** Draw a 1 px outline only on the outer silhouette (the hexagon), in a darker shade of the object's own base colour, not black, so it reads at 1x on a 150 px canvas without looking heavy after nearest-neighbour upscaling; separate the three faces by value alone (no interior lines). Add a 1 px highlight along the top-left edge of the top face (the edge L–B) in the top colour lightened again. On a 16x8 grid a 1 px outline eats a quarter of a tile's height, so skip outlines on floor-level items (rug, shadow) and keep them for boxes only. These outline rules are the author's inference from pixel-art practice; no fetchable source stated them.
- **Object recipes (all from boxes and diamonds).** Bed: 2x1 footprint, box `h = 8`, top face split into a mattress (lighter) and a pillow (1x0.4 tile diamond at the back end, white, 2 px tall box); headboard: a thin box (0.15 tile deep, `h = 14`) along the back edge, drawn via the same routine. Table: 1x1 or 2x1 top slab box of `h = 2` at `z0 = 10`, plus four 1 px legs (vertical lines from the slab's bottom corners down to the floor projection of each corner). Chair: 1x1 seat box `h = 6` at `z0 = 4`, back slab 0.15 tile deep `h = 10` along the back edge, legs as lines. Shelf: 1x0.3 box at `z0 = 18` against a wall plus a back-plate rectangle painted on the wall layer. Lamp: a 1 px (or 2 px) vertical post from the tile centre to `z = 20`, a 0.6x0.6 box shade at `z0 = 18`, `h = 6`, and a soft light: fill a diamond on the floor around the lamp with the floor colour lightened. Plant: pot = 0.5x0.5 box `h = 5`; foliage = three overlapping filled circles/ellipses of two greens above it (circle radii 3-5 px), still sorted as one box. Rug: a flat union of tile diamonds in two colours (border = outer ring of the diamond test with threshold 1 - 2/H), drawn in the floor layer. Picture frame: in wall space (see Q4) a rectangle of size 8x6 wall-pixels with a 1 px frame, mapped to a parallelogram on screen. Window: a 10x12 wall-space rectangle filled with the `Sky.phase` colours, a 1 px frame and a 1 px cross bar; draw the window and frame before the skirting so nothing overlaps them.
- **Shadows.** A flat diamond (the footprint shrunk by 0.1 tile, offset by `(+1, +1)` px away from the light) in the floor colour darkened 15-20 %, drawn in the floor layer so it never occludes. For round objects (pet, plant) a filled ellipse `rx = W/4`, `ry = H/4` under the feet, drawn in the floor layer at the pet's current position.

### Gaps
- No fetched source gives numeric lightness offsets for the three faces or outline colour rules; the figures above are recommendations, not cited values.

## Key question 4: A convincing 2:1 room in about 150x110 px: tile size, room size, wall height, walls, skirting, window, clipping

### Takeaway
At 150x110 the only tile sizes that fit a 6-7 tile room with walls are 16x8 (7x7 floor = 112x56) and 20x10 (6x6 floor = 120x60); 24x12 leaves no room for walls beyond 5x5. Artists report that larger tiles (24x12, 36x18, 48x24) give more detail but look less like pixel art, and 16x8 is the smallest common 2:1 tile. Wall height has no published rule; 3-4 tile heights (28-40 px) leaves the pet (34 px sprite) room to stand in front of the wall without clipping into the ceiling.

### Cited Findings
- Tile sizes: an artist describes working at 24x12, 36x18 and 48x24, noting "the bigger the size, the more details you can put into, but the less it looks like pixel art"; any size that keeps the 2:1 ratio works — [Clip Studio Tips](https://tips.clip-studio.com/fr-fr/articles/4969?org=1). 16x8 is the full viewable tile at the smallest common size — [OpenGameArt](https://opengameart.org/node/5374). The GameDev.net classic uses 32x16 — [article744](https://archive.gamedev.net/archive/reference/articles/article744.html). Habbo-compatible renderer: 64 px per tile zoomed in, 32 zoomed out — [nitro-renderer](https://github.com/billsonnn/nitro-renderer/blob/dev/src/room/utils/RoomGeometry.ts).
- Isometric furniture asset packs are commonly cropped to "work well on 64 by 32 pixel tiles" — [OpenGameArt Isometric Furniture](https://opengameart.org/content/isometric-furniture).
- Tutorials recommend building a template first so pieces fit together — [Construct, Creating Isometric Pixel Art From Scratch](https://construct.net/en/tutorials/creating-isometric-pixel-art-355/page-5).

### Inferences (numbers for this canvas)
- **Floor bounding box** for an `n x n` room = `n*W` wide by `n*H` tall. On 150x110:
  - 16x8, 7x7: 112 x 56. With a 32 px wall and a 4 px front skirt/edge: total 92 px tall, 112 wide; margins 19 px each side, 18 px above. Pet sprite (41x34) spans ~2.5 tiles wide, ~4 tile heights tall. Recommended default.
  - 20x10, 6x6: 120 x 60; wall 36 px -> 100 px tall, 15 px side margins. Chunkier furniture, 36 tiles, pet ~2 tiles wide. Good alternative if 49 tiles feel too fine.
  - 24x12, 6x6: 144 x 72, only 3 px side margins -> no room for wall thickness; 5x5 (120 x 60) fits but gives 25 tiles.
  - 32x16, 4x4: 128 x 64 fits but furniture becomes 1-2 pieces per room.
- **Room origin.** For a 16x8 7x7 room centred horizontally: `ox = (150 - 112) / 2 + 56 = 75` (top vertex x), `oy = 110 - 4 - 56 - 1 ≈ 49` so that the front vertex sits at `y = 105` and the walls (`hWall = 32`) rise to `y = 17`, leaving 17 px of ceiling/background above. Vertical placement can be tuned to leave room for the sprite's head when it stands on the back row: the back tile's centre is at `oy + 4 = 53`, sprite top = `53 - 34 = 19`, inside the wall, fine.
- **Walls.** Left wall: the parallelogram with vertical sides spanning the edge from `B = P(0, 0)` to `L = P(0, n)`, height `hWall` up; a point at wall-space `(u, v)` (u along the wall in tiles, v up in px) maps to `sx = ox - u*W/2`, `sy = oy + u*H/2 - v`. Right wall: along `B` to `R = P(n, 0)`: `sx = ox + u*W/2`, `sy = oy + u*H/2 - v`. Fill column-wise: for `x` in the wall's x range, `yFloor(x)` from the edge formula, fill `yFloor - hWall .. yFloor`. Give the two walls different values (left lit, right shaded, same as the box faces). A 1 px vertical corner line at `x = B.x` in the darker wall shade reads as the corner. Optional wall thickness: extend each wall 2-3 px outward past `L` and `R` as a lighter cap (the "top of the wall") to suggest depth; this is the 19 px side margin's main use.
- **Skirting.** 2 px (16x8) or 3 px (20x10) band at the bottom of each wall following the same 2:1 edge (`yFloor - skirt .. yFloor`) in a wood/white colour; draw after wall decorations so a frame never hangs below it, before the floor so the floor's tips overwrite the band's bottom row cleanly (or draw it after the floor if the band should sit on top of the floor edge; either is consistent as long as it is one choice).
- **Window.** A rectangle in wall space, e.g. `u in 1.0..2.6` tiles, `v in 12..26` px on the left wall for 16x8; because `sy` depends on `u` the window's horizontal edges are 2:1 lines and the vertical sides are vertical, so fill it column-wise like the wall. Only put windows and frames at `u` such that `u*W/2` is an integer (multiples of 1/8 tile for W=16) so corners land on pixels.
- **Clipping.** Clip every primitive to the canvas rectangle in the column loop (`y0 = max(y0, 0)`, `y1 = min(y1, canvasH)`), and clip floor objects to the floor's x range only where they could extend past the walls (they cannot if footprints are inside the room). Nothing else needs clipping because sorting handles occlusion.

### Gaps
- No source gives a wall-height convention for small iso rooms (Habbo's wall height in tile units was not found). The 3-4 tile-height figure is a layout inference.
- No source discusses 150x110-class canvases specifically; the numbers above are arithmetic on the cited tile sizes.

## Key question 5: Picking objects by touch

### Takeaway
Convert the touch to canvas pixels (divide by the upscale factor), hit-test objects in reverse draw order against their drawn pixels, and fall back to the floor tile from the inverse formula; the published inverse formulas work once the origin is anchored at the tile vertex and pixel centres are used.

### Cited Findings
- Inverse formulas and the half-tile pitfall: see Q1 cited findings ([GameDev.net](https://gamedev.net/forums/topic/642767-i39m-desperate-isometric-mouse-coordinates), [Parallel Realities](https://parallelrealities.co.uk/tutorials/isometric/isometric2.php)); the tutorial's reason for rounding with doubles was that "integer maths will fail due to needing to round half up".
- Sprite anchor: character ground point should sit exactly on tile intersections or the misplacement makes characters look like they hover — [sprite-ai.art](https://www.sprite-ai.art/guides/isometric-pixel-art); a Godot thread places a sprite on a 32x16 grid at the diamond centre `(16, 8)` — [Godot forum](https://forum.godotengine.org/t/understanding-tilemap-positions-and-sprite-rendering/138646); asset authors recommend the sprite origin "between the feet" because code tracks that point — [itch.io comment](https://zerie.itch.io/tiny-rpg-character-asset-pack/comments?after=128).

### Inferences (recommended procedure)
- **Touch to canvas.** `px = floor((touchX - viewLeft) / scale)`, `py = floor((touchY - viewTop) / scale)` where `scale` is the nearest-neighbour factor (e.g. the Compose canvas width / 150). Reject if outside `0 until 150 x 0 until 110`.
- **Id buffer (exact, cheap).** While drawing each floor object, also write its index into a parallel `IntArray(150*110)` (`-1` = nothing, `-2` = floor, `-3` = left wall, ...). Picking is then `ids[py * 150 + px]` in O(1) and exact to the silhouette, including tall or overhanging parts, with the topmost (last drawn) object winning automatically. On this canvas the extra buffer is 16.5 K ints. Enlarge small items for touch by also testing the 8 neighbours or a 3x3 to 5x5 block around the touch and picking the most frequent non-floor id.
- **Without an id buffer.** Iterate objects in reverse draw order; test the touch against each object's screen bounding box (computed from `L.x .. R.x` and `B.y - h .. F.y`), then against its hexagon (Lebron's three-axis test, with the touch as a point) for precision.
- **Floor fallback / "move here".** If no object is hit: `a = (px + 0.5 - ox)/(W/2)`, `b = (py + 0.5 - oy)/(H/2)`, `tx = floor((a+b)/2)`, `ty = floor((b-a)/2)`; valid if `0 <= tx < n && 0 <= ty < n`. For drag-to-move furniture, snap the dragged footprint's back corner to the tile under the finger minus half the footprint, and refuse (show red) when the footprint leaves the floor or overlaps another footprint (which also keeps the sort well-defined, see Q2).

### Gaps
- No cited source on touch-target enlargement or id-buffer picking for iso scenes; these are standard-practice inferences.

## Key question 6: Animating a front-facing sprite walking across iso tiles (mapping a wander position, feet anchoring)

### Takeaway
Keep the wander position as fractional tile coordinates, project it with the same tile-to-screen formula as the floor, and draw the sprite with its origin at the feet (bottom-centre), so a straight-line walk in tile space is a 2:1 line on screen; sources agree the origin belongs at the feet but disagree on whether "on a tile" means the diamond centre or the bottom vertex.

### Cited Findings
- Origin at the feet / bottom centre so spawn points and flipping line up — [itch.io](https://itch.io/post/16655038), [zerie.itch.io comments](https://zerie.itch.io/tiny-rpg-character-asset-pack/comments?after=128). Diamond-centre placement at `(16, 8)` for a 32x16 tile — [Godot forum](https://forum.godotengine.org/t/understanding-tilemap-positions-and-sprite-rendering/138646). Ground point on tile intersections (vertices) in another guide — [sprite-ai.art](https://www.sprite-ai.art/guides/isometric-pixel-art). Tiled anchors tiles at the bottom-left by default — [Tiled forum](https://discourse.mapeditor.org/t/is-there-a-way-to-set-tiles-pivot-point-to-center/2578).

### Inferences (recommended procedure)
- **Position model.** Store the pet at `(px, py)` in continuous tile units (`0 <= px, py <= n`), with the diamond *centre* convention: tile `(tx, ty)` centre is `(tx + 0.5, ty + 0.5)`. Screen feet point: `fx = ox + (px - py) * W/2`, `fy = oy + (px + py) * H/2` (round to int once, after the full computation, to avoid 1 px jitter). Draw the sprite at `(fx - spriteW/2, fy - spriteH + footInset)`, where `footInset` is 1-2 px of the sprite's bottom rows that visually sit "on" the ground (the chibi's feet occupy the last rows; use the image's own height, not a constant, since rabbits are taller).
- **Walking.** The wander/`PetBrain` state machine already moves a 2D position; interpret its x/y as tile units scaled so one tile = one step unit. A walk of `d` tiles along +x moves `(d*W/2, d*H/2)` px on screen (a 2:1 line, no jaggies); along +x+y it moves straight down `d*H`; along +x-y straight right `d*W`. Walk speed in tiles/sec should be chosen so the on-screen speed stays >= 1 px per frame on the slowest axis (at 16x8, 0.25 tiles per frame along x moves 2 px right and 1 px down per frame).
- **Depth.** The pet's draw order is decided by its box in the topological sort (Q2): because `py` is fractional the pet smoothly passes behind a table when `py + 0.35 <= table.y0` and in front when `table.y1 <= py - 0.35`. To avoid a visible pop, keep the walkable mask one tile clear of tall furniture so the transition happens while the sprite is partly hidden by nothing, or accept the pop (Habbo-style rooms do).
- **Facing.** A front-facing sprite on a 2:1 floor is the Habbo-style compromise: the 4 iso walking directions map to 2 drawn directions (left/right flips) in the simplest version; a "back view" when walking up-screen is an optional extra. No source was needed for this; it is the design consequence of having only front-facing frames.
- **Shadow.** Flat ellipse (`rx = W/4`, `ry = H/4`) centred at the feet point, in the floor layer, updated every frame.

### Gaps
- Sources conflict on vertex vs centre anchoring; the choice above is a convention, not a cited fact.
- No source covers mixing a front-facing (non-isometric) sprite with a 2:1 floor; the compromise described is the author's inference from Habbo-like games.

## Key question 7: How hobby/indie engines do "pixel-perfect isometric" and the common pitfalls

### Takeaway
Habbo-compatible renderers (nitro-renderer, scuti, chroma4j) use a 64 px (zoomed-in) / 32 px (zoomed-out) tile scale, project with rotated basis vectors and compute depth by a dot product, while pixel artists keep to the 2:1 grid, nearest filtering and exact integer corners. The recurring pitfalls across sources are: thin seams from sub-pixel misalignment or filtering, jaggies from 30-degree lines, wrong occlusion from single-key sorting (including cycles), and floor+wall combined sprites hiding objects.

### Cited Findings
- Habbo-compatible renderer constants and projection (64 / 32 scale, `scale * sqrt(0.5)`, z compressed by `sqrt(1/2)/sqrt(3/4)`, depth dot product with clip range -500..500) — [nitro-renderer RoomGeometry.ts](https://github.com/billsonnn/nitro-renderer/blob/dev/src/room/utils/RoomGeometry.ts). Scuti renderer (TypeScript, PixiJS) covers "Tiles, Stairs, Walls, Materials, Landscapes" and both wall and floor furniture — [scuti-renderer](https://github.com/mathishouis/scuti-renderer); chroma4j renders Habbo furni SWFs with state, direction/rotation, colour, shadow options — [chroma4j](https://github.com/Quackster/chroma4j). A bobba-engine parser stores Habbo-style tile heights 0..31 — [bobba-engine-parser](https://github.com/Hester60/bobba-engine-parser).
- Seams: nearest filtering fixes seams for pixel art; high-fidelity tiles need oversizing with a one-pixel fringe — [Godot forum](https://forum.godotengine.org/t/thin-lines-between-2d-isometric-tiles-nearest-fixes-pixel-art-but-no-filter-works-for-high-fidelity-tiles/119606); one pixel of corner misalignment produces visible seams — [sprite-ai.art](https://www.sprite-ai.art/guides/isometric-pixel-art); a Unity user with gaps between diamond sprites was told to match pixels-per-unit to the sprite resolution — [Unity discussions](https://discussions.unity.com/t/a-little-help-with-creating-and-using-tile-palettes-for-an-isometric-game/1714940).
- Jaggies: true 30-degree lines look jagged; 2:1 (26.565 degrees) is used instead — [Clip Studio Tips](https://tips.clip-studio.com/zh-tw/articles/4969?org=1), [SLYNYRD Pixelblog 41](https://slynyrd.com/blog/2022/11/28/pixelblog-41-isometric-pixel-art).
- Z-fighting / ordering: single-key failures for 1x2 objects ([GameDev.net](https://gamedev.net/forums/topic/425769/)); cycles of three mutually overlapping blocks have no valid order and must be split or clipped ([Lebron](https://github.com/shaunlebron/IsometricBlocks/blob/master/index.htm)); Unity's default sort axis ignores Z and needs a bias ([Unity Manual](https://docs.unity3d.com/es/2021.1/Manual/Tilemap-Isometric-CreateIso.html)).
- Objects overlapping walls: sprites that combine floor base and wall (Kenney tiles) make y-sorting hide objects; "there is no escape from obscuring some part of the world" in pure screen order — [GameDev.net 2025](https://gamedev.net/forums/topic/719280-isometric-tile-render-order).

### Inferences
- For a procedural renderer the three "pixel-perfect" guarantees reduce to: integer tile anchors with even `H`; every edge drawn as exact 2:1 runs (2 px per row) via pixel-centre tests; and no texture sampling at all (the buffer is upscaled once with nearest-neighbour, so the Godot/Unity filtering class of seams cannot occur).
- Pixel Dungeon was named in the brief, but I found no source tying it to isometric rendering; to my knowledge it is a top-down (non-iso) tile game, so its relevant lesson is the oblique/top-down alternative in Q8 rather than iso conventions. Treat this as unverified.

### Gaps
- Habbo's exact furniture sort key and wall height were not found in any fetchable source.
- SLYNYRD's and Lospec's pixel rules could only be read as search excerpts.

## Key question 8: The alternative: oblique / cabinet projection and when it is easier on a tiny canvas

### Takeaway
Cabinet projection draws the front face straight on and recedes depth at 45 degrees at half scale; Ultima VII is the usual game example and is described as oblique with halved depth. It is rarely used because the two visible side walls are unequal and symmetric objects need extra angles, but for a single-back-wall room on a tiny canvas it is simpler: walls, frames and windows are axis-aligned rectangles, depth sorting is plain y-order, and a front-facing sprite is in-projection.

### Cited Findings
- Cabinet projection: front face straight on, depth receding at 45 degrees at half scale; projection direction about 63.4 degrees to the picture plane, depth edges at half their true length — [Netmath lexicon, cabinet projection](https://lexique.netmath.ca/en/cabinet-projection/); [DevDungeon, Cabinet Oblique Drawing](https://cathy.devdungeon.com/post/cabinet-oblique-drawing.html).
- "The Ultima VII perspective is an oblique projection with halved depth commonly called cabinet projection"; it is "rarely used because it produces unequal sides for the bottom and right walls, which can mean drawing four angles instead of two for a symmetrical object"; the variant works on a 16x16 grid rather than 32x32 and tile editors may force placing wall pieces by hand — [OpenGameArt, Perspective Walls Template thread](https://opengameart.org/content/perspective-walls-template). Another commenter says Ultima VII uses a 1:1 px diagonal and that they tried 2:1 to reduce skew; another describes it as a "-135 oblique projection" — [OpenGameArt comment](https://opengameart.org/comment/40398), [Reverse Engineering Ultima VII isometrics](https://lpc.opengameart.org/node/3356).
- Bellanger's oblique template: base tile 32x32 within a 64x64 tile; walls 16 px thick, doors 8 px thick; walls line up to stack on layers — [OpenGameArt, Perspective Walls Template](https://opengameart.org/content/perspective-walls-template). An "Oblique projection: all assets" collection exists on the same site — [LPC oblique assets](https://lpc.opengameart.org/node/13617). The chart "Isometric games don't exist" catalogues the projections games actually use — [cyangmou, itch.io](https://cyangmou.itch.io/isometric-games-dont-exist).

### Inferences (numbers for this canvas)
- **Cabinet floor mapping.** Tile size `T` px square in front view; depth halved: tile `(tx, ty)` (ty = depth, 0 at the back) has its front-left corner at `sx = ox + tx*T + (n - ty) * T/2`... more simply, with `ty` increasing toward the viewer and the back wall at depth row `n`: `sx = ox + tx*T + ty*(T/2)`, `sy = oy + ty*(T/2)` for a 45-degree recession to the right; the floor is a parallelogram `n*T + n*T/2` wide and `n*T/2` tall. For 150x110 with `T = 12`, `n = 6`: floor 108 x 36, back wall 72 x (say) 40 px, a 2:1 recession (`sy` step `T/4`, `sx` step `T/2`) flattens it further. A 1:1 diagonal (Ultima VII) gives perfect 45-degree pixel staircases; a 2:1 one gives the familiar iso staircase.
- **Why it is easier for a tiny canvas.** One back wall is a plain rectangle, so the window, picture frame, shelf and clock are axis-aligned rectangles (no parallelogram mapping, no odd corner pixels). Furniture is a front rectangle plus a top parallelogram (and optionally a right-side parallelogram), i.e. two fills instead of a column-wise three-face box. Depth sorting is exact by `ty` (then by `tx`) for footprints that do not overlap, because every object's front face is at a single depth; multi-tile objects pose no problem. The pet's front-facing sprite is in-projection, and it reads at a glance like Pokémon / Stardew-style "3/4 view" rooms that players already know. Text and UI badges on objects stay readable.
- **When to prefer 2:1 iso instead.** Two visible walls (more "dollhouse" charm, and the brand's existing room may already be read as a corner room), the floor diamond fills the canvas more evenly, and furniture turned 90 degrees mirrors cleanly (the OpenGameArt thread's complaint that cabinet needs extra angles for symmetric objects is real: a chair facing right and facing left are different drawings in cabinet, mirror images in 2:1 iso).
- **A third, even simpler option** (not a cited source; standard top-down "3/4" practice): a flat top-down floor grid with a straight back wall, sorted by bottom-edge y. It loses depth cues on the floor but every primitive is a rectangle; it suits widgets smaller than ~100 px.

### Gaps
- No pixel-art tutorial was found specifically for an oblique/cabinet *room*; the geometry is from general drawing references and game-art forum discussion.
- The claim that Ultima VII's walls are perfectly vertical with a 45-degree floor is a community description, not confirmed from Origin documentation.
